AI for Healthcare: survival analysis

Same recipe, different question: logistic loss vs Cox partial likelihood

Both models do the same thing: write down the probability of what actually happened, take −log, and push β to make that number small. Cross-entropy is a negative log-likelihood. The difference is the question each probability answers:

  • Logistic regression: did this patient die in hospital, yes or no? One probability per patient, time is ignored
  • Cox: if one death happens at this moment, how likely is it to be this patient and not another one still in the ICU? One probability per death, time order matters

Start with the worked example, then move the sliders and try the three experiments. Every symbol is explained at the bottom, in "How the numbers are calculated".

The one idea to take home

Logistic regression asks one yes/no question per patient: did this patient die? Cox asks one "who?" question per death: among everyone still in the ICU, who dies now? The model gives each of them a chance (their risk score divided by the total of all the scores). The penalty is −log of the chance it gave to the patient who really died. The penalty formula is the same in both models. Only the question changes.

In ML terms: Cox applies a softmax (scores turned into chances that add up to 100%) over the risk set, then cross-entropy on the patient who died. Logistic regression applies a sigmoid, then cross-entropy on died or not.

Try this in class (5 to 7 minutes)

1. What does each model learn?

  1. Press the button. Both β go to 0, so age has no effect
  2. Read the worked example: each model gives patient A a penalty
  3. Move a β slider and watch the probabilities and penalties change
  4. Press Fit in each panel. The loss falls to its lowest point

Takeaway: both models learn β by making the total penalty as small as possible. They define the penalty differently

2. Why does time matter?

  1. Press the button. Patient A's death moves from day 3 to day 20. Nobody's outcome changes
  2. The logistic loss stays the same
  3. The Cox loss changes, because who was still at risk at each death has changed

Takeaway: Cox uses the order of events, logistic regression ignores it

3. Why do the coefficients differ?

  1. Press the button to fit both models on the same 9 patients
  2. Compare the two best β and the two e^β
  3. Logistic gives an odds ratio, Cox gives a hazard ratio. They are different quantities, so do not compare them as if they were equal

Takeaway: the same patients and the same predictor give different β, because each model minimises a different penalty

Only the order matters

Double every stay length. The order of deaths and discharges stays the same, so both losses stay identical. This holds for the partial likelihood, which Cox uses to learn β. The full Cox model still depends on time: the baseline hazard and the survival curves use the actual days.

Limits of this demo

  • Toy data with one predictor. The notebook fits a multivariable Cox model with penalizer=0.01 (small L2 penalty) and Efron handling of tied death days. This page uses no penalty and the simpler Breslow convention for ties: everyone with day ≥ t is at risk
  • The two loss values are sums over different things (9 patients vs the number of deaths), so do not compare their sizes. Compare where each curve has its minimum
  • Discharge alive is a competing event. Treating it as censoring, as here and in the notebook's Cox model, estimates the cause-specific hazard of in-hospital death, not the absolute probability of dying. That is why the notebook adds the cumulative incidence function (CIF)
  • Only the order of events enters the partial likelihood. The baseline hazard and the survival curves still depend on the actual time scale
  • The partial likelihood does not estimate the baseline hazard λ₀(t). lifelines estimates it afterwards (Breslow estimator) to turn hazard ratios into survival curves
